# kk-interface

The interface here is everything the hand does: what a person can do, where they find it, how they do it, what answers them, and how they take it back. Each interaction gets the place, the form and the care its weight earns, and the weight has five parts: **frequency and reach**, **effort**, **impact**, **confidence** and **mission value**. And the hand learns: what keeps its place and meaning becomes a habit and costs nothing; whatever moves or changes meaning costs something every time. So every principle is checked the way people really act, the twentieth time, on autopilot, in the middle of something else, not the way the flow was drawn. The aim is that attention stays on the person's task, never on operating the interface.

The sources are the Bureau Gorbunov's interface school, the research on memory, habit and action, and KK's cases and comments, kept here as principles; take the principle from them, never the picture. kk-1337 holds the laws of the hand and time (pointing, paths, seconds per job, the limits of waiting, target sizes) and the way of thinking around this skill; the words on controls are kk-typography's, how things move is kk-motion's, and the look is kk-look's.

## The weight of an interaction

List the jobs people do here, each from the moment the need arises to the moment it is resolved, and weigh each one, from data first (logs, commits, history, their own words), otherwise by a stated assumption. Each part decides something:

- **Frequency and reach**: how often the job is done, and by how many. Repetition in a stable place makes a habit, so frequency decides the place (near, visible and constant for the frequent; one step away for the occasional; deeper for the rare), the fast path, and how quiet the answer is: a job done a hundred times a day gets no question and no ceremony. Reach decides whose path it is on: a job everyone meets once explains itself; a job a few do daily is dense and fast, and stays out of everyone else's way.
- **Effort**: what the job costs each time, in steps, pointing, moves between keyboard and mouse, decisions, waiting, memory and errors. Count it in steps and seconds and lower it where frequency and reach are highest; a change pays only when the seconds it saves, times every time the job is done, outweigh its price in building, learning and broken habits.
- **Impact**: what changes when the job goes right or wrong (data, money, other people, time), and whether it can be taken back. A costly mistake gets prevention, undo, a delay or a step of its own; a valuable success gets a prominent place even when rare; a harmless job gets speed and silence.
- **Confidence**: how much of this is measured. A heavy decision resting on a guess gets one quick check first (the data, a timed walk through the job, a prototype of the step); a light one goes ahead with the guess stated.
- **Mission value**: how close the interaction is to what the product is for. The interaction the product exists for earns the most direct form, made of what is already on the screen, and the product's one novelty; the periphery (signing in, settings, administration) stays standard and cheap. Invention spent on the periphery is too much; the core job done with a stock control nobody remembers is too little.

When the parts disagree, don't average them: a rare job with high impact is placed like a frequent one, a frequent harmless job loses every question and flourish, and a job far from the mission may lose its place however often it is asked for.

## Where it lives

- **Frequency sets the place.** Controls are grouped by function and ordered by frequency: the frequent visible, constant and nearest to where the hand and the eyes already are; the occasional one step away; the rare deeper; what is needed now and then appears when needed. Permanent space for the rare is too much, and a crowded bar is a cue to remove, not to shrink; a hidden step (under More, on hover or right-click only) for something needed every time is too little.
- **Places stay put.** People learn where things are and stop looking (kk-1337: search becomes knowing), so nothing they reach from memory is reordered by recency, popularity or state: the unavailable turns pale where it is, a pair of buttons keeps one order across the product and the platform, because the hand presses by position without reading, and a new version keeps the old places unless the gain is several-fold. Freezing what nobody has learned yet is too much.
- **One role, one object.** Name the roles (go somewhere, find, ask, filter, create, decide, undo) and what serves each. Two elements for one role split the habit; one element with two roles is a mode. What fights for one place, or does one job, is one thing: merge it all the way, so one object takes the role, its rare options live inside it (typed, or one step away), and its answer shows on the content itself, not in a second list. The decision on a planning board: finding, going, filtering, asking and creating became one field, and the board narrows as you type. It flips when the content can't carry the answer (no spatial layout, too many items); then a list of results is the smaller construction. Asking an agent is one of these roles, in the same object: asking about a thing picks up its last conversation, and what the agent changes shows where it happened and what changed, and is undone like the person's own action.
- **The controls are one layer** above the content, which is the figure: as few objects as there are roles, grouped by function and ordered by frequency, with a clear edge where they meet the content and nothing unreadable beneath them.
- **A control lives next to what it changes.** A setting sits by the thing it drives, grouped by meaning rather than storage, and a value has one home: a second place is at most a read-only reflection. Frequent commands go where the hand already is, on the object or in a menu opened there, and the facts a decision needs stand at the control that takes it.
- **The content can be the control.** When the screen is already shaped like the task, the content takes the action: the item is dragged to where it goes, the list narrows where it stands, the text is edited where it is read. A control and a step of translation disappear. It flips where the content can't show the result.
- **Depth in place.** Detail opens where the person is, level inside level: the levels above stay on top as the way back, the rest stays in view, deeper levels appear only when opened. A page with a way back, to change one thing, costs a round trip every time; a big, self-contained task (a full editor, a long setup) gets a place of its own, with the way back obvious.

## The element by its function

- **The job decides the family:** choosing one of several or several, on or off, entering a value, setting or exploring a range, a command, going somewhere, composing a list, a process. A family is one choice in different forms (how it reads as a phrase is kk-typography's), and the forms differ in price: whether the options show without an action, the room they take, the hand they suit, when the consequences come, whether they can be taken back.
  - *One of several:* short options that fit a row after editing, a segmented control, whose single container says "only one"; long options with room, a radio column; little room or many options, a dropdown, whose options hide until it opens and which alone may stay empty. A picker wheel is for touch and a predictable sequence. For a date, the days in view beat a hidden list; a far or exact date is faster typed.
  - *Several:* a column of checkboxes, or on touch a list with checkmarks and no row fill; not a list selected with modifier keys, where one plain click throws the whole choice away.
  - *On or off:* a checkbox in a form when "off" is obvious (read the label with "not" in front); a switch when it applies at once; two named options when "off" is unclear. Yes-and-no options are a checkbox in disguise.
  - *A range, or exploring what the options do:* a slider that changes the result as it moves, with a field for precision; minus and plus steps can't dose the rate of change, and a "Calculate" button hides how the values depend on each other.
  - *A command:* a button; several, a menu; commands on a thing, a menu opened at it. *Going somewhere:* a link, never a dropdown.
  - *Composing the list itself:* an editable list (edited in place, reordered, an empty last row to add by typing), or two lists to move items between.
  - *A process:* one form for whoever does it often; a wizard only for a rare, linear task everyone does once, which shows how many steps there are, keeps every answer through going back and changing branches, and checks all that success needs before it lets the person go.
- **In a form, or at once.** Radio buttons and checkboxes wait for the form's command; a segmented control and a switch may act at once, where flipping back and forth starts nothing irreversible and no process.
- **A form is a promise.** People read an element by its form before its words, so it does exactly what its form says, and what looks pressable is pressable, as a whole: a box says "type or tap here", a chevron "this leads elsewhere", an icon its behaviour, pale "unavailable" and nothing else. A broken promise is a lie; a mark that promises nothing is noise. The decision in a dense panel used daily, where every row opened in place: no chevrons, no boxes around values. It flips when a row really leads elsewhere, when the fold is news to the person, and on touch or for newcomers, where nothing at rest would show what can be tapped.
- **The default decides.** Most people do what is preselected. The recommended option comes first unless a natural order puts it elsewhere; where no default is acceptable (a test, a survey, gender), "not specified" is selected, since a group left empty can never return to empty.
- **Name the rejected neighbour** and try the hard cases: two options, disabled, empty, the longest option, a narrow screen, a finger and a mouse.

## Buttons, links and fields

- **A link goes, a button does.** Going somewhere is a link and behaves like one (a new tab, the way back); an action is a button; what each says is kk-typography's. Buttons come in few kinds (main, secondary, dangerous), each drawn one way across the product.
- **Press is not commit.** The look changes at the press, the action fires on release over the same element, sliding away cancels, and a second press during processing does nothing twice. A double click may differ from a single only when the single is its first half (select, then open); otherwise every single click waits. A press or a drag never selects text by accident: only text that is edited or copied is selectable.
- **The default button carries out the intention**, never the "safe" cancel, and the best question is the one a default removes (renaming a file selects the name without its extension).
- **Fields.** Data is asked for when the person has a reason to give it, in stages across their use, and nothing the system knows or can work out is asked. A field keeps its label once filled; a placeholder is only an example. Spaces, hyphens, brackets and a plus are normalised, not refused; a mask is allowed only if copying, pasting and undo survive it; on a phone, the field calls the keyboard its data needs and never zooms the page. A value is checked as it is entered, the error sits in the field, and a longer form shows what is missing instead of refusing silently.

## Gestures, modes and dragging

- **One gesture, one meaning.** A mode is a state in which the same gesture does something else while the person isn't attending to it; errors follow, habit makes them certain, and they are the interface's fault. The fixes, best first: separate the gestures (two results, two controls); a quasimode, a state that lasts only while held (a key, a press, a drag); change the scenario (the object first, then the action; the parameter after the action, its result visible). Showing the state where the person looks is a compromise: fewer errors, but attention leaves the task and no habit forms. Different behaviour in different places is no mode when the place is in the person's attention. A gesture fixed inside a part can become a mode of the whole (a map in a scrolling page that takes the wheel), so check one level up.
- **Nothing changes under the hand.** No control changes meaning as the person reaches for it, nothing shifts under the pointer while things load, and a one-off mode resets after use.
- **The thing follows the hand.** The whole object is the grab area, and a drag starts past a small threshold, so a slightly moving click stays a click; then the object stays exactly where it was taken, with no lag even when the content isn't ready (an approximation now, the exact result later). Only the places that can take it respond, the landing place shows before the drop, and what the drop needs exists only while the hand moves: guides, the length, a grid in the domain's units, a container that opens when held over, even a view that changes to the target's scale and returns when the drag ends. The result follows the movement, never waiting for release or "OK". Dropping where nothing can take it, or Esc, puts it back; after the drop, undo. How it moves is kk-motion's.
- **Never only a gesture.** Every drag, swipe, long press and hover has a visible way that does the same: a menu, a button, the keyboard.

## The answer: states and feedback

- **Three lines of dialogue.** Before the action, what can be done (the element's look, hover, a visible focus, the places a drag can land); during it, "I hear you" (the pressed look, the echo of typing, the lit target); after it, what is happening and how it ended, with the way on.
- **At once, where it happened, as quietly as will do.** Something answers at once even when the result takes longer: after about two seconds of silence people decide it didn't work and do it again. The answer comes where the action happened, in the quietest channel that is enough: the element itself, the pointer, text beside it, motion, sound or vibration, and last a modal window, never for routine or a small slip, since it teaches people not to read windows. A no is gentle, and often the absence of a yes is enough. Whatever answers must survive its hundredth repetition.
- **Accepted is not done.** Success is announced only once it has happened; an instant "accepted" is honest only if a failure follows quickly and visibly. A process may be paced to feel faster; its outcome is never faked.
- **Indicators by what is known.** A short process or an unknown share: a spinner in the element that started it. A background process with a usable partial result: a small spinner by that result. A long one: the share, "X of Y", the time left, rounded so it doesn't jump, a sign of life while the share stands still, and a timeout if it can hang. The element the process belongs to becomes its indicator before a new one is added.
- **States are messages:** neutral, hover, pressed, focus, disabled, selected, mixed, error, loading, receiving a drop, visited, empty. Each has its own means, one meaning per means across the product (pale never means both "disabled" and "hint"; one highlight shape serves every reason to highlight), and states that coexist use independent means, so the pair still reads. A state's weight follows its significance: only the selected segment is filled, the current menu item is brighter than its neighbours and not a link, the inactive dims and never vanishes. No state promises what won't happen: nothing disabled or already selected answers hover or a click. Focus and visited are signal, not clutter. A state is entered at once.
- **Every state makes sense on its own.** People work in fits and starts and come back midway, so each screen and each self-contained part is clear in every state, even one that lasts a second, without the steps before it: a change of state keeps its context, and the sparse version still reads. An empty place holds the action that fills it, or fills itself with something useful, and one that would stay empty is not shown. A loading place keeps the shape of what is coming. When nothing matches, the content stays, dimmed, so the person sees that nothing fits. An error keeps what the person did and puts the way on in place: try again, or an alternative. What each of them says is kk-typography's.

## When it goes wrong

- **Prevent rather than report.** What can't work right now is unavailable, looks it, and has the way to enable it at hand; input is corrected or normalised rather than refused; a page doesn't link to itself. "That didn't work" after the fact irritates by its timing, however gently it is said.
- **Undo instead of confirmation.** Confirmations, red warnings and "Don't show again" are passed on autopilot, and a barrier moves attention to itself instead of the decision. So do it at once and let it be taken back: a notice that blocks nothing and shows what was done, then deeper layers (history, a bin that empties itself). An error must be seen to be undone. What is physically irreversible becomes reversible through a delay (sending) or by editing and deleting afterwards. When neither is possible and the impact is high, the decision earns a deliberate step, a question of its own or a press held until it fills, rare enough to keep its signal (what it says is kk-typography's). A question before anything reversible is too much; a silent irreversible act is too little.
- **The person's data is never lost.** Nothing entered disappears: going back, switching a branch, an error, a crash, a return after a break. A draft waits where it was left.

## Moving through: navigation, menus, dialogs

- **Every screen answers four questions:** where am I, where can I go, what is there, how do I get back. The current place is marked, brighter than its neighbours, and doesn't link to itself; a section the person is inside, below its top, is marked and leads to its top. The way back is always obvious and returns them exactly where they were: the scroll, the selection, the open item.
- **Scent, not clicks.** The count of clicks predicts nothing; each step must show the person they are getting closer: a preview, the very values they came for. Before adding a level, remove the ones that only lead elsewhere, and build around the person's own objects, so one structure serves every surface.
- **A menu is a learned image.** People find items by their order, groups, marks and shortcuts, so items keep their places in every state, and none repeats what a click on the screen already does. The whole row is the target; a submenu opens on hover, with room to travel diagonally toward it; items choosing one of several are marked unlike on-off items. A context menu opens at its thing; press, slide and release chooses in one gesture; Esc or a click outside closes it; commands keep their traditional places even in an unusual product. A menu for two commands that fit on the screen is too much; the command used every time, only in a menu, is too little.
- **A modal window is for a decision that must come now.** A task that blocks the rest dims what it covers; a task that runs alongside opens beside it without dimming, and the flow stays. A sheet or a popover opens next to what opened it and offers what this moment needs, not every option for every case; Esc or a click outside closes anything without unsaved work; focus moves in and comes back. A window for a result that could show in place is too much; a destructive decision hidden in a hover is too little.

## Lists and tables as controls

- **Choosing is not selecting.** Choosing sets a value; selecting picks objects to act on. An element that chooses values doesn't borrow selection's modifier clicks, and a list of objects marks what is selected so that no row fill outshines the mark.
- **The objects first, then the action.** Rows are selected, then acted on from a bar over the selection: no tool is picked in advance, and many are handled as easily as one.
- **The row is the unit.** Rows are objects and columns their properties; the name comes first, the whole row is the target, and its detail opens under it. Columns and their order follow frequency; on a narrow screen they go by significance, and the rest opens in the row. Nothing jumps: rows keep their places while the person works, widths hold, and the order changes only when asked. How a table is set is kk-typography's.
- **Finding, sorting and filtering are one object**, with the rare filters inside it and the result on the list itself, with a way to clear it.

## Several things at once

- **One change, one event.** Everything an action touches answers together (the button, the counter, the target), so the person sees one event, not three; how they move together is kk-motion's.
- **Keep the incompatible apart in time.** Heavy work runs in the pauses, not while the person types; nothing appears over the task in hand: a notification, a tour or a prompt waits for a pause.
- **The next step at the peak.** The next useful action is offered at the moment the person has a reason for it: right after a success, beside a result. Offered before that, it is noise.

## Every hand

- **Pointer.** Hover shows what can be done and is never the only way to anything. The first tooltip waits a moment, so a passing pointer doesn't set it off; its neighbours then open at once.
- **Touch.** Nothing hovers, so what can be tapped looks tappable at rest; the answer comes at the touch; the frequent and the main actions sit within the thumb's reach, low on a phone, finger-sized with room around them. Swipes, long presses and pinches are shortcuts with a visible way beside them, and a swipe never fires a tap.
- **Keyboard.** Everything the pointer does, the keys do: a visible focus in a sensible order, Enter for the default, Esc to cancel or close, arrows within a group. Frequent actions get shortcuts, shown wherever the command lives, so the fast path is learned from the slow one; a temporary tool is held on a key, not switched on.
- **One product for every hand.** Ask what the input can do (hover, a fine pointer), never guess the device; a screen reader gets each control's name, role and state; colour never carries a state alone. For the same job, the platform's conventions win: its back gesture, sheets, menu bar and button order. What an emulator can't show (sticky hover, a keyboard over the field, the reach of a thumb, a swipe on glass) is tried on a real device.

## From the job to the detail

The order is part of the craft: the jobs and their weights first, each carried from the need to "resolved", every click answered by its next frame; then the places, the objects that serve the roles and what opens where; then the elements, chosen by function against their neighbours; then their states and answers; and the nuances last: thresholds, shortcuts, delays, the focus order. When a nuance won't work, go back a level: a mode that needs indicating is a scenario to change, and a control that needs explaining is in the wrong place or form.

## How you know

- **Weigh the jobs** on paper, each with its five parts, and check that its place, form and care follow from its weight, every assumption marked.
- **Count the seconds.** Write each frequent job as steps and seconds, before and after; a change pays only across every time the job is done.
- **Do it the twentieth time**, fast and on autopilot: where does the hand land, which question gets clicked through, what did Enter do?
- **A table of gestures and states** for every main control, one meaning per gesture; then press, slide away and release (nothing fires), and press twice fast (nothing happens twice).
- **Render every state** of each control and screen, including the longest real value, no match, and a return after a break: one means, one meaning.
- **Count objects per role** in the controls layer, and the round trips (open, change, back) per frequent job.
- **Keyboard only, then a real phone:** every job done without a mouse (in a daily tool, the frequent work never leaves the keyboard), every target reached by a thumb, every swipe tried, nothing hidden behind hover.
- **Drag everything that drags:** it stays with the hand, only the valid places answer, the landing shows before the drop, Esc cancels, undo works. The measured check is kk-motion's.

## What went wrong before

Seen in agents' work on KK's products:
- Rows that opened in place kept their chevrons after he had asked for none, an arrow beside a minus read as arithmetic, and a bare bar, the digits 1 2 3 and a stray marker made him ask what they were.
- One job got two homes: a value with a knob and a field, two context menus on one object, a new column for overdue goals while the inbox already held what waits to be reconsidered.
- Search, navigation and filters stayed three controls for one role, a selector used almost never took permanent space, and the agent answered in a panel of its own. United, the first version was still a switch, a field with a list and an agent button; the answer belonged on the board.
- One setting took a page with a back button and three taps; a reschedule dialog offered every option for every case; a "Move to" list held 205 goals, where the board could be the list and a drag the move; a menu mixed unrelated verbs with one a click already did.
- Options were drawn as screens with the interaction left out: what a click on "Other" does, how the thing actually moves.
- Drags had a small grab area and never showed where the card would land; swipes shipped untried on a phone, with false taps and fields that zoomed.
- A click left a black border and selected a headline's text, and hover lit a card and its children as separate shapes of different widths.
- A search with no match emptied the board, and a stuck state offered no way to start again.
