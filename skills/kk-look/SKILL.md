# kk-look

The look is the product's soul made visible. The eye takes in differences before anything else: every difference in size, weight, value, colour, shape, texture or depth says "this matters" or "these belong apart". So the look rests on one budget, **difference**. Spend a few strong ones on what the product is like and on what matters now, give every other difference one job, and remove the rest. What is left is quiet, familiar and the same everywhere, and against that quiet the soul and the main thing are unmistakable. Beauty comes from the same budget: clarity is processed with ease, ease feels beautiful, and one surprise, placed where it means something, is what people remember.

KK's bar is "Bold, clean, swiss and stylish thoughtful design with parts that are truly beautiful and unique." Swiss here means the fundamentals under every look (a grid, hierarchy by size and weight, flat colour, real pictures, nothing without a job), never a style to put on; the soul decides the rest. The sources are KK's own cases, comments and taste index, the research on beauty and on visual variables, and the fundamentals of the Bureau Gorbunov and of the platforms, kept here as principles. How text is set is kk-typography's, what the hand does is kk-interface's, and how things move is kk-motion's; kk-1337 is the way of thinking around them all.

## The soul

- **Name it before any form.** In three to five words, say what the product is like as a person, and what it is not. It comes from facts: the owner's words, the product's name and promise, the audience's world, and the moment it is used in, such as a phone at night or a printout on a desk. What it is not names the nearest wrong soul (competent, not corporate) and rules out that look.
- **An existing product already has one.** Read it from its name, its words, its brand and the screens people love, and extend it; a part takes the soul of its whole. Changing a soul is a decision about the product, and it is the owner's.
- **No brand yet.** Derive the soul from the audience and the job, and mark it as an assumption. When two souls are genuinely possible, they go to the owner as options, side by side.
- **Every choice of look answers to it.** For a face, a colour, a texture, a picture or a radius, say which part of the soul it carries. A choice that carries none is a default, so take the plainest familiar option instead.
- **The look and the words are one person.** Whatever the voice is (frank, calm, cheeky), the look says it too; the words themselves are kk-typography's. Humour can carry the soul while the numbers carry the trust.

## Contrasts carry it

- **Two or three strong contrasts, and everything else quiet.** The axes are size, weight, value, colour, density (empty against full), shape (sharp against round), texture (flat against a photo, 3D or a drawing), tone (plain facts against a joke), time (still against moving) and era (old craft against a modern grid). Attention goes to novelty times significance (kk-1337), so contrast is novelty to spend: on the soul and on the main thing.
- **Strong or none.** A contrast that carries the soul is unmistakable: several times the size, black against white, a real object against flat type. Every other difference is the smallest one that is still clearly seen (Tufte 1997), or nothing. Two sizes a hair apart, two near greys, a second accent, radii of 4 and 6 px: the eye asks "is this different?" and gets no answer.
- **Air is a contrast.** A heavy sentence on an empty screen weighs more than the same sentence on a full one, and the emptiness around the main thing is part of its weight.
- **One hero per screen** carries the size contrast: the name, number or statement the person came for, several times the body size, with everything else small and quiet. In a dense tool the hero can be a column of numbers. How it is set and anchored is kk-typography's.
- **Weight follows the attention needed.** The state that asks the person to act gets the fill and the large figures; the routine states stay plain.
- **Where it turns to too much.** Add a fourth strong contrast, or use one strong contrast on many things, and nothing stands out; many contrasting tiers fragment a page as much as many similar ones.

## Colour

- **Black, white and greys carry most of it.** The structure is built in value and size, so the hierarchy holds in grayscale, and colour adds meaning on top, never structure. A meaning carried by hue alone is lost in grayscale and to colour-blind eyes, so it also gets a word, a shape or a place.
- **Grey is a budget too.** Body text is at full contrast, and grey is kept for what is secondary and for states. When the whole interface is grey, grey has nothing left to say, and important text in pale grey is a sign of an immature design. The floors are 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for large text and for the parts of controls (WCAG); a grey that encodes a state may be quieter on purpose, and still reads where it is meant to be read.
- **Each colour has one job and one meaning in the whole product:** the action, a state, today, the brand, a category, because one colour on two unrelated things links them (kk-typography: alike is one). The meaning is the product's own contract, kept everywhere, charts and emails included, and not colour psychology: "blue means trust" does not replicate, while colour's reliable jobs are perceptual, to pop out, to group and to tell kinds apart (Elliot & Maier 2014).
- **One accent,** for the main action or the one thing to look at now. It both highlights and groups, and it works only while it is rare, so count its uses per view.
- **A bare control needs a strict sign.** The less a control looks like its kind, a link that is only coloured text or a button that is only a label, the more strictly its colour is kept: that colour means "you can press this" everywhere, and nothing else wears it.
- **Colours of meaning** (a state, a change, a category) are few, a handful with their key in sight. In a dense view they are pale tints, so a full day stays calm, and full strength is only for what needs acting on now. A colour's force grows with its area: what must be seen at a glance tints the whole row, and a detail takes a dot.
- **What colour can say.** Hue tells kinds apart but cannot rank: order goes by value, size or position, and a quantity runs from light to dark in one hue, never a rainbow. For amounts the eye judges position on a shared baseline best, then length, angle and area, and colour intensity worst (Bertin 1967; Cleveland & McGill 1984), so a comparison that matters shares a baseline.
- **Colour lives in the objects, not the shell.** The interface can stay black and white while the colour comes from the photos, the 3D things, the products' own icons and the data.
- **Colour sits in flat areas:** a button, a header, a tint. A gradient appears only where it is the soul's own light, and a signature has conditions: a gradient mark that works large and central turns flat, white or one colour, when it is small or at the edge.
- **The ground is white or black by default,** with full text contrast. A tinted ground needs a job, such as a desk under a white document; a warm or cream ground "for mood" lowers the contrast and says nothing.
- **Dark is the same soul in another light.** The greys and the accent are tuned again for the dark ground, never simply inverted.

## The typeface follows the soul

- **Choose the face from the soul,** and say which part of the soul it carries. A tool or an instrument takes a grotesk, and inside a platform's app the system face, the platform's own voice. Something literary, crafted, historic or theatrical takes a serif or a crafted display face. Data where alignment is the point takes tabular figures, or a monospace. A brand voice takes a display face for the hero only, over a quiet text face.
- **Few.** Two faces at most, and weights far apart rather than close. One family in one weight is a whole system when the soul is plain and loud, and then the hierarchy comes from size and tone alone. KK's measure is not how minimal a page looks but "how strict we are with the amount of elements and fonts".
- **A face chosen without the soul is a default,** in either direction: one neutral sans on everything, or a serif "for elegance" on a tool. In a product with a system, the system's face wins, and a favourite face laid over it breaks the product's look.
- **It must work:** every language the product speaks, Cyrillic included; tabular figures; the weights the levels need; clean rendering at the smallest size used.
- How the face is set, from sizes and levels to x-height and leading, is kk-typography's.

## Texture, material and depth

- **Texture is a contrast of surfaces:** flat type against a real photo, a 3D object, a drawing, an engraving or handwriting. One idea of texture, done finely, and the rest flat.
- **A material is taken for its function, never its fashion.** Under every fashionable material lies a fundamental: the controls are one layer above the content, what floats shows that it floats, and nested shapes share a centre. Take that, and leave the glass, the glow and the gloss. "Search for fundamentals, don't bate for the stylisation."
- **Depth says what is above what.** What sits on the page casts no shadow; what floats over it, a menu, a sheet, a toast or a card in the hand, gets one level of depth, a little heavier for a bigger surface and over a busy ground. A modal dims what is behind it; a panel beside the work leaves the work live. Two translucent layers are never stacked, and text over a picture or a blur gets a solid backing (kk-typography).
- **Few cues are enough.** An outline, an overlap or a hint of perspective gives volume, and realistic light and shade is rarely needed. A change of colour often does the job of a shadow.

## Pictures

- **A picture only where it carries something:** the product itself, the real object, the real person, the data made visible, a metaphor made real, an explanation faster than words, or a comparison every reader must judge the same way. KK: "Photos yes why not, but only if necessary, never decoration."
- **A picture is the strongest claim on attention.** Pictures outrank headlines, logos and text, so a picture becomes the anchor wherever it stands: it gets the place of the main thing or of the soul, and no other.
- **Real, or made from the real.** The product, its real screens and data, real photos and objects. An agent may make a picture (a render, a model, a drawing) from that real material, and type can be the picture: a word or a figure set large. Where nothing real exists yet, type becomes the picture, or a marked slot waits with a one-line brief. Never an invented stand-in, a stock photo or a mood image.
- **Faithful and unambiguous.** A picture of a real thing looks exactly like it. People find faces and creatures in shapes, so check what a newcomer sees in each one.
- **One way of picturing across the product,** chosen from the soul: one kind of photo (its light, crop and distance), one way of drawing, one kind of 3D. A series keeps one angle, one light and one line weight.

## Icons and marks

- **An icon only where it is recognised faster than a word:** a standard meaning, a repeated command, a tight space. Anywhere else a word, and an icon people might not know gets its label. An icon is a promise about what happens (kk-interface), so it is chosen by the meaning people already hold, not by its looks.
- **One set in one style:** one stroke weight, one corner, one grid and size, and one logic of fill, where outline and solid mean one thing, such as selected. Inside a platform, the platform's own set. A mixed or misaligned set reads as carelessness.
- **In a set, the differences show.** What repeats in every icon goes: a circle or a tile around each merges them into one texture.
- **A live icon keeps a constant part.** An icon that shows real data, the time, a count or a state, keeps one fixed part, so people still recognise it when its content changes.
- **A mark keeps its promise.** An arrow promises a move elsewhere, and a chevron a page or an unfold; a mark whose promise is not kept is noise, and an arrow next to a number joins the arithmetic. Glyphs come from the right set: true ↑ and ↓, and a round dot rather than a long dash as a bullet.

## Few values

- **Every distinct value has a job:** each colour, grey, radius, shadow, stroke width and fill. Near-duplicates go first. A starting budget is one or two radii, one level of shadow, one stroke width, one accent and a handful of colour meanings, and every extra value names its job. Sizes and spacing are kk-typography's.
- **Shape follows role.** A button, a tag, a status and a card each have their shape, and one shape means one role; pills on everything erase the differences. On touch platforms, soft continuous corners are the idiom.
- **Corners describe the group.** A group rounds at its outer corners and keeps its inner joins straight, so it reads as one object. Nested shapes share a centre: the inner radius is the outer one minus the padding.
- **One means of separation per edge.** Space first; where space is not enough, one of a line, a fill or a shadow, never several. A card with a border, a shadow and a fill says the same thing three times.
- **A fill competes with what it holds.** A fill behind every value makes a panel heavy and breaks it into boxes. It flips where people must see where to type or tap before they try (kk-interface: touch), and where a thing is plainly a card: then a light fill reads better than a ghost outline, and stays quieter than its content.
- **Scaffolding whispers.** Most of the ink is content. Grids, rules, frames, axes and legends are the lightest marks on the screen, just above invisible, so the content carries the contrast, and ink that neither informs nor structures goes (Tufte 1983, 1990).

## Beauty is ease, with one novelty

- **Beauty is partly ease.** What the eye processes fluently, a clear figure and ground, symmetry, contrast, repetition and a typical form, feels beautiful and true (Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004). So clarity is half of beauty, and decoration is no part of it: the right impression comes from knowing exactly what to add, never from frills.
- **Typical, with one novelty.** People prefer the most advanced form that is still clearly its kind, and typicality and novelty suppress each other (Hekkert et al. 2003). Keep the kind familiar, and put the novelty in one place where it means something.
- **The first glance** judges beauty within 17–50 ms, by visual complexity and by how typical the form is for its kind, so fewer big shapes and a familiar kind win (Tuch et al. 2012).
- **Beauty earns the first try, and the work earns the rest** (Tractinsky et al. 2000; Hassenzahl 2004). It never costs reading, and it never covers for a thing that does not work.
- **Finish is removal.** Polishing removes noise and sharpens differences; it never adds effects. It is simplicity, not minimalism: hiding everything looks minimal and is not simple. Every value is a choice you can defend, and delight is what getting the rest right feels like, not confetti on top. Stripping goes too far when the removed part carried something, such as a box that said "tap here", or when the people it is for read the bare thing as cheap or broken.

## The one memorable element

- **The ideal is one signature idea per product,** and it carries the soul. It means something: the metaphor made real, where the name, the line and the gesture are one idea. It works: it carries the data or does a job. And it is flawless, or it is absent, because a show that glitches is remembered for the glitch. It may come back wherever it means something.
- **A few unique parts on one page are fine** when each one means something and works.
- **Everywhere else, beauty is a question, never a quota.** Could there be beauty here? If yes, does it come from the nature of the task, the content and the surface, or only from our pushing? Keep only the natural kind; pushed beauty is decoration.
- **Where it lives.** It arrives after the main thing has been read, and everything around it is quiet so it can shine. What is admired once may be clever, and what is read fast and often must be plain: a figure-ground trick is wit in a logo and interference in a control.
- **Playful is welcome when it means something,** and character comes in moderation: an overdose of it becomes noise. Whether and how the element moves is kk-motion's.

## One look across the product

- **One soul everywhere:** every page, state, theme, width and platform, and everything the product sends, such as an email, a PDF or a deck. Never generic in one half and good in the other; count the styles across screens, not per screen.
- **The product's own system is its look.** Its components, faces, colours and radii are reused as they are, never redrawn as look-alikes, and a fix lands in the component, so it lands everywhere. A new part takes the look of its kind and should "simply feel like ours".
- **Coordination is combing, not cloning.** Things of one kind share their means (colour, shape, radius, line, icon style, the angle and light of pictures) and differ only where they mean something different. The same look promises the same behaviour (kk-1337: one form, one meaning).
- **The platform's look is part of the system.** On a phone or tablet, people's habits reach down to the platform's system face, its large titles, its soft continuous corners, even the character of its shadows: take its fundamentals, never its fashion. On the web the same confidence carries over: big type, big controls, one hero, bare tables. Which controls and conventions to use is kk-interface's, and when to leave the system is kk-1337's.

## The borrowed looks

A vague "avoid a generic look" swaps one default for another; naming the defaults works. Each is a default, not a choice, and each flips only when you can name the job it does here.
- **The Linear look:** dark glowing gradients, purple-blue gradients, neon glow, glass cards, bento grids, tiny grey type, the same premium dark look on every product. The glow and the glass decorate the shell and compete with the content, and tiny grey type loses the signal. A dark ground is fine when the soul or the moment is dark, a night, a stage, an instrument, with sizes people can read.
- **A fashionable material as a style:** Liquid Glass, glassmorphism, neumorphism, gloss, noise and grain. Take its fundamental instead.
- **Book typography on a tool** ("too much bureau"): a serif text face, thin rules between facts, paragraph indents, a book measure, one document frame for every kind of content. It flips where the product is a text to read.
- **The model's own defaults:** a cream or warm off-white ground; an italic accent word in a heading; 01 / 02 / 03 section labels; monospace or spaced capital labels over every block; pill buttons and badges everywhere; an icon in a circle on every item; emoji as bullets; large radii on everything; everything centred.
- **Loud emphasis:** red and capitals. Red is a meaning (an error, a loss) and appears only for it, and emphasis comes from the product's own contrasts, size first.
- **The average of galleries, and another product's look.** "Don't mimic apple. Lead with true sense." A reference passes KK's bar and the owner before anything is taken from it, and what is taken is its thinking, never its surface or its signature.

## From the soul down

The order is part of it: the soul in words before any form, then the two or three contrasts, then the face and the palette, then texture, pictures and icons, and the few values last. The memorable element is found on the way, in the nature of the task, never added at the end to make it nice. When a choice and the soul disagree, change the choice; the soul itself changes only by the owner's decision.

## How you know

- **Name the soul from the render alone,** as a newcomer, in three to five words. It should match yours.
- **List the contrasts** that carry it: two or three strong ones. Find every weak difference left, and make it strong or take it out.
- **Grey it, then blur it.** In grayscale the hierarchy holds and no meaning disappears; blurred, a few big shapes remain, the hero and the element stand out in the order they matter, and nothing else competes.
- **Count the values** in the audit: every colour, grey, radius, shadow, border, face and weight, each with its job, and how many can go. For colour, also the accent's uses per view and the contrast of the text and of the parts of controls.
- **Line up every screen** at thumbnail size: the pages, the states, both themes, the phone and the desktop, and whatever the product sends. One look, and no generic half.
- **Try the element everywhere it appears:** every state, width and theme, and with reduced motion. It means something, it works and it is flawless, or it goes.
- **Check the borrowed looks** on the render, not in the code.
- **Hold it against the taste index** (kk-taste, and the owner's own when they keep one): take the entries that solve the same problem, and ask whether this reaches their level, never whether it looks like them.

`node scripts/snap.cjs <file or url>`, from kk-1337's folder, renders the screens as a person scrolls, the blurred and grey views, and an audit of every distinct colour, background, radius, shadow and border, and of the sizes and faces; `--dark` adds the dark theme and `--thorough` the other devices.

## What went wrong before

Seen in agents' work on KK's products:
- New pages came out in the model's default look instead of the product's own: "Why do I see the shitty slop web pages instead of the kk design system based web pages?" A page asked for in the product's own style came back with capitals used nowhere else in it, and another ignored the styling agreed earlier in the same session.
- Parts looked like the system's and were not: a button unlike any in the system ("We don't have such buttons anywhere. Rounding is wrong here."), a border that made the top buttons "look really weird", components over-complicated next to the simple ones already in the gallery. The fix belonged in the component.
- A borrowed loud style: red and capitals in a report, "НЕ НАША" (not ours), and an all-caps kicker over a headline.
- The look broke in the middle: a document "generic at the first half and then suddenly becomes cool at the second half", a catalogue set differently from the report it came from, half the screens of one app good and half sloppy. His fix was to count, on every screen, the elements, the contrasts and the styles.
- Decoration with no job: a dark grey fill behind every field ("made design much worse"), black borders on clicked cards, a blur behind widgets that should have been solid black, a black stroke on a few dots that read as a distortion, and a second background effect beside the one that worked.
- Contrast misjudged both ways: inactive cards faded until they vanished, when they only needed to be a little darker than the active one; labels and figures whose contrast was "too low".
- Marks with no job or from the wrong set: arrows next to numbers and on rows that open in place, after he had asked for none; stray arrow glyphs instead of ↑ and ↓; a long grey dash as a bullet where a black dot looks sharp; a hover icon that looked bad.
- Pictures that failed: text set straight on a photo so it could not be read, a QR code with half its squares missing, particles that looked like worms.
- A signature outside its conditions: the gradient logo works "only if it's in the center and big"; at the edge it has to be white or salmon.
