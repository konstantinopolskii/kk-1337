# kk-shaping

Shaping is how a task gets understood deeply enough that its answers can be drawn. It lives inside the first phase of kk-1337's cycle, the quick alignment and then the bold marker sketches of whole flows, and adds no steps of its own: it sharpens what goes into the alignment and decides what the sketches show. It comes back only at the last stage, when the build starts, to cut the work into pieces and hand it over. It rests on one ratio, **the most good for the person for the least new construction**, found at **the moment the job breaks for them** and checked against **how they cope today**. The best answers are made of what is already there.

The sources are the Bureau Gorbunov's school of problem solving, Ryan Singer's Shape Up and KK's own cases and comments, kept here as principles. kk-1337 holds the cycle, the alignment message and how the sketches are drawn; kk-interface holds what the hand does, kk-typography the words, kk-look the look.

## The moment it breaks

- **The request is a true goal and a guessed method.** Keep the goal and its limits, set the method aside, and look again: the answer is rarely where the request points.
- **Ask when, not why.** When did they last need it, what were they doing, where did it break, how did they get by? "Why" brings a theory and "what should it look like" a design; "when" brings back a real moment with its cost. Keep the "why?" for yourself, asked of the problem until the answer is obvious.
- **The story is the test.** One specific story of where today's way breaks, and how people get by: their workarounds show the need, and often the form, better than any request. Swap every option into the story; the ending must be clearly better. Stories that break in different places are different problems, one flow each.
- **The good, without the method.** Name the useful action, what the thing exists for: the good this person needs at this moment, in words that name no solution, since a method inside the statement narrows the search to variations of itself. Name the harm beside it (time, errors, doubt, lost trust, attention spent on the machine), and what matters most to them now: speed, certainty, calm, status or money.
- **What it's worth, the appetite.** How often the moment comes × how much it matters × what it costs them today (kk-1337's formula) says whether it earns a quick fix, a flow or a new direction, or nothing at all. Propose it in the alignment as an assumption the owner corrects: how big a change, how many of their rounds. The size shapes the kind of answer: at a small appetite the best answer is a different one, not a smaller copy of the big one, and what bends is the scope, never the quality.
- **Estimate the way work goes with agents.** Building is now the cheap, fast part; the owner's attention, the agreeing and the checking are the scarce ones, and estimates from human-team habit come out wrong. Take the numbers from the owner's own record of planned against actual, where they keep one; none are written here, and with no fresh record, say the estimate is uncalibrated.
- **Say a dead end once, with a way out**: a simpler option drawn, and the price of the mistake in what the decider weighs. Repeating the argument is noise; reframe it. The call stays the owner's.

## The conflict

- **Say it as "X, but Y".** It must show everything, but fit a phone; quick every day, but clear the first time. The conflict is where the problem lives, and stated sharply it decides most of the design.
- **Invent, don't split.** Giving up drops one need, force pours in money, screens or people, and a compromise makes both sides a little worse. Demand both in full.
- **Test the wall.** Most limits that block the best answer are habits in the head: a screen that "must" hold everything, a function that "needs" its own element. Ask who set each limit and what happens if it goes.
- **Give each side its own moment**: all the room while it is in use and back after, or only where, or only when, it is needed. A trade that survives every attempt (freedom against guidance, power against ease) is decided by who the answer is for, never blended.
- **New good must not cost the old.** People who don't use the new thing see nothing change, and every habit they really have still works.

## What is already there

- **Inventory before invention**: the data and content the system holds, its components and working mechanisms, the platform's abilities, the history and usage, people's habits, words and workarounds. Extend what already works before inventing a second way to do the same thing, and change only the part that fails.
- **The best resources are free**, and an answer built on them costs almost nothing, which is what elegance is.
- **Familiar forms are a resource.** Known patterns and common words are understood at once; a new form must earn the learning it costs.
- **The answer usually exists somewhere.** With the good, the harm and the conflict named, search by those exact demands, often in a neighbouring field. A trite answer that meets them is a good one; unusualness is not a criterion.

## The ideal and the moves

- **Ideally, the job is done and nothing new exists.** Hand the function to something already there, so the element that would have done it never appears: the content becomes the control, the existing flow carries the new choice. Ask of every new place, state, setting or concept what would do its job if it were forbidden. It stops being ideal when that part starts to mean two things, or to act differently in a state the person doesn't notice.
- **The ideal steers; today is the floor.** Name the ideal out loud, with the step back you'd accept if it costs too much. Aim at it while searching, so the first workable idea doesn't end the search; judge the result against how people cope today, so the work can end.
- **Where the leap comes from:**
  - the entry point: start where the need arises, not where the feature lives;
  - the level: up to the bigger system, where a difference is possible, or down to the one part that carries the trouble;
  - what the product does: do the work for the person (compute it, fill it in, move the waiting out of sight, remember it for them);
  - the product's next step: richer, a new principle, adapting, running by itself, or back to simpler;
  - the workaround: make what people do by hand the feature;
  - the breaking point: one line where the harm happens instead of a system around it.
- **A small fix and a ×10 move pass one test**: the good per unit of construction. A line at the point of harm that saves a month of building is a move; a new system that saves a click is not.
- **Go up for a difference.** When everyone at a level promises the same, the difference is one level up, in the person's bigger goal, where a clear stand is both new and important to them.

## From the understanding to the sketches

- **Options come from different moves**, not tints of one idea: the familiar pattern, the ideal, another entry point or level, and the owner's own idea as they said it. Two tints of one idea are one option.
- **Each option is one sentence first**: to let this person [have this good], we [do this], because [this makes it work]. If it needs "and also", it is two options; if it has no "because", nobody knows why it would work; a part nobody can name plainly means the idea is still muddled.
- **Walk it in words before drawing**: the places, what one can do in each, and where each action leads, from the need to "resolved". Only the connections matter here, not the form; the words stay yours, and the sketch shows the result.
- **Solved, not just clearer.** Every option answers how the work actually moves, and says what it deliberately doesn't do. Showing the problem more clearly is not an answer.
- **Must-have or nice-to-have**, for our own judgement. A must-have is what the answer can't work without for the story; a nice-to-have is anything it can ship without, kept only if there is room. Every idea that comes up meets the same questions: can it ship without this? Is it a new problem, or one people already live with? How often, for whom, at what cost?
- **Rough everywhere but the linchpin**, the part everyone must see to get the idea: any detail drawn early becomes an instruction.
- **Close the holes before the owner sees it.** In kk-1337's walk through each flow, go state by state and through every neighbour it touches. A part you can't answer is a hole: patch it with the simplest answer that keeps the core, fence the case out, or cut it; never leave it for later, because one hole can multiply the work.
- **Count what each adds** (places, states, modes, settings, concepts, words, screen space) against what it reuses. The better flow usually removes the question instead of answering it.

## Off the screen

- **The same thinking, another material.** A service, a positioning, a business model or a team's process is shaped from the same story, good, conflict and resources; its places are wherever a person meets it (a call, a message, a desk, a payment), and the breaks tend to hide in the handoffs between people.
- **The page can be the by-product.** When the request is a page or its words, the task is often the model underneath: what this is, for whom, and why it is worth it.
- **A process is a product too**: find where the work piles up or leaks, and start at the narrowest point, not everywhere at once.

## When the build starts

The last stage of kk-1337's cycle, once the design is agreed: the work is cut and handed over here, never earlier.

- **Hand over the whole shaped piece, never a task list.** It carries the story, the one sentence, the agreed design as the north star, the must-haves, what it deliberately doesn't do, and the holes already closed; the builder finds the real tasks by doing the work. Where the brief lives is kk-1337's.
- **Cut it by its anatomy.** Pieces that can each be finished and shown on their own (create, find, send, undo), never layers or roles (markup, styles, copy; design, code): only whole pieces show progress. The real pieces show up after the first real work; redraw one you can't tell how done it is, one whose name would fit any project ("front end", "fixes"), or one too big to finish soon.
- **One piece done early**: the core, small enough to finish soon, and new if two are equal, built end to end so it can be tried.
- **Uphill, then downhill.** A piece is uphill while its approach is still being worked out, downhill once only making is left. Push the unknowns over the top first, because easy work fills whatever time it is given. Solved means tried with your hands, not thought through; a piece stuck uphill is several pieces, or a hole the shaping missed.
- **Stop against the story.** It is done when, for the main case, it is clearly better than how people cope today, the core works and its craft is right. "Not ideal" doesn't block it; "no better than today" and "the core is broken" do. Unknowns left when the time is spent mean the shaping missed something: reshape, don't push.

## On point

The result is short. The alignment carries the understanding in a few lines (the moment, the good, the conflict, what it's worth) and only the questions that change the picture; the sketches carry the answers. No write-ups of the digging: every line either changes a decision or goes.

## How you know

- **Swap it into the story**: the ending is clearly better, without squinting.
- **Say it in one sentence**, with a "because".
- **Walk it** from the need to "resolved", including the way out and the limits: every action leads somewhere, every "what if…" has an answer, every neighbour still works.
- **Count what it adds** against what it reuses: each new thing needs a job nothing old could take, and it costs everyone who never uses it.
- **The final questions.** Why will it work? Does it break anything that worked? Will people notice it where they need it, and understand it at first sight? Is it almost free?

## What went wrong before

Seen in agents' work on KK's products:
- A part was solved while the task it belonged to was bigger: one field was redesigned on its own, while he meant how everything is found and filtered, now, ahead and behind.
- A symptom was polished and the job behind it left alone: a late date got a clearer label, while why plans slip went unasked.
- A clearer view of the problem was offered as its solution: related facts side by side, and no answer to how the work moves. "A good solution for the problem solves it explicitly."
- An option needed its own processing for each kind of case, so its cost would grow with every new kind: "We search for simple solutions, I would say systematic and universal ones."
- His "what if" was built word for word within the hour, before agreeing on what the thing is: "You jumped ahead too early and treated it too directly."
- A reference was found and its idea built on before the problem was agreed with him.
- A whole control centre was drafted where a sparkline carried the information: "For what?"
- The digging went "a little bit too far", and answers came long, as reports and documents, when he wanted them short, on point and drawn.
- Estimates came in the terms of human teams, wrong for work done with agents.
