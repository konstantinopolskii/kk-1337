# kk-typography

Typography here is everything where text is involved, in any text people read, from a button to a message, a post, a report or a page: what the text says, how it is set, and where it sits. The saying rests on one ratio, **the share of meaning in the message**; the placing rests on two variables, **distance** and **visual mass**. The words come first and the form carries them; and since the eye groups before it reads and sees ink, not boxes, every principle is checked on what is read and seen, not on the intent or the code. The aim is that the reader spends attention on the content, never on decoding it.

The sources are the school of the Bureau Gorbunov (interface text, headlines, numbers, informativeness, layout), Ilyakhov's information style, Sarycheva's editing method, Feynman's plain explaining, Lebedev's Kovodstvo, Payasu's optical strings, the gestalt principles, the research on reading, and KK's own comments on words, kept here as principles. Their examples are old: take the principle, never the picture.

## The reader and the job

A text carries meaning to one reader at one moment, and it works when a first-time reader gets it at a glance, from the words alone, and believes it. So the work runs from meaning to words: the reader and the job, the facts, the whole, the word, the sound; and every edit keeps the author's meaning.

- **Name the reader.** Who reads, what they already know, what they feel right now: in a hurry, afraid of losing something, choosing. A newcomer needs the terms and the order, an expert the nuances and the limits, and a text for both bores one and leaves the other empty. With no reader named, write for a smart person with no context, the Feynman way: a teenager's words, an adult's calm tone, and no drift back into jargon as a conversation grows long.
- **Say what changes after reading**: a decision, a step, a new view, a feeling. "To inform" is not a job. If one sentence won't say whom the text is for, what changes for them and what it stands on, no wording will save it.
- **The place sets the register.** A label, a status, a message, a reply in a conversation, a post, a report and a page differ in length, directness and address. A strict register (reference, documentation, a report) states facts in the third person and gives no orders; an author's register (a post, a letter) speaks in the first person, with a voice and a position. Code, commands and settings keep their exact form, and a warning before something that can't be undone stays exact and explicit: plain words never soften it.
- **Words are written in their place**: in the design, in the real slot at its real length, and judged there. A slot has a length budget, and the words are edited to fit it, never the type shrunk. The copy lives in one place; a second copy in a document drifts from the live one.

## Facts before words

- **A text is made of facts.** Numbers, names, dates, cases, sources and how the thing works make it deep and believable. An evaluation ("convenient", "unique", "a serious problem") pushes a conclusion on the reader; the fact behind it persuades more, because the reader draws the conclusion. Remove the evaluation: if nothing is lost, it was noise; if something is, find the fact.
- **Never invent a fact.** A made-up price, number, quote, review, client, result, date or behaviour of the system looks exactly like a real one, which makes it the costliest mistake in text. A missing fact is a visible placeholder or a question to the author, a number you lack is an open question or a marked estimate, and an open question never goes out as a settled rule. The pull is strongest where a pattern has a slot to fill, like the way back in a toast or how an empty place gets filled: without the fact, the slot is a question to the author.
- **Every claim shows its grade and its edge.** A fact, an observation, a conclusion and a hypothesis stay apart. "Many", "increasingly", "people want" hide a number, a source or the edge of what was seen: give the number or the source, or say there is no more. One case is one case.
- **A promise carries its proof**: a fact of the product, a demonstration, a case with its task and its price, an honest limit on whom it is not for. A result without its price reads as an advert, with it as experience; advice without its first step is noise the reader has heard before.
- **The content before the phrasing.** Words may change until the end; the facts and the structure are right first. Polish makes an error more convincing, and a text that needs endless polishing is missing a thought, not words.

## Say it once

- **Informativeness is the share of meaning in the message.** The channel to a person is narrow, so every word, mark and repeat either carries meaning or spends their attention for nothing. There are two levers: add signal (the value instead of the field's name, the price on the button, the object instead of its type icon) and remove noise.
- **Each fact once per view.** A fact shown twice makes the reader check whether the two are the same, and the second copy tells no one anything. A repeat earns its place only where the reader can't see the first one when they need it: another screen, a new printed page, far down a long scroll.
- **Factor out what repeats.** A word, unit, month, type, status or verb that repeats in every row goes once into the heading of the group or column, and the freed heading can then carry signal of its own.
- **The context has already said it.** The screen, the heading, the section, the unit, the value itself and the element's nature need no words: no label for what speaks for itself, no word the heading already holds. The context is what the reader sees right there, never what the author knows about the product. A value that could be taken for another of its kind is the exception: it gets a label that tells them apart.
- **No recap.** A message doesn't retell the reader's own situation or explain its previous sentence, and no summary repeats what was just read.
- **Data takes the most visible place.** The first line of a message, a notification, a subject line, a card or a row goes to the facts the person came for, never to formalities.
- **Give a new job to an existing element** before adding one: progress inside the button that started it, a state inside the control it changes. A combination of states is one sign, not two.
- **The shortest version that keeps the meaning.** Then read it as a newcomer, and bring back anything needed that went out with the noise.

## The whole

- **One thought** per text, post, message, paragraph and control, said in one sentence before writing: I want to show that X, because Y. A second thought gets a place of its own: the button acts, the heading names the state, the text beside them gives the details.
- **The answer first.** The first line, the subject line, the first screen and the first sentence of a paragraph carry what the reader came for, and the reason follows. Scanning eyes land on the first words of lines and items, so the meaning goes there.
- **The reader's questions, where they arise.** What people actually ask, the awkward question included (the catch, the cost, what if it fails), is answered in a plain statement where the question arises, set to be read, never buried in a list at the bottom or in small grey type.
- **The order is the reader's**: what it is, what it gives them, the proof, the next step; never who it is for before what it is. An enumeration is set as a list.
- **Length follows the job.** A line meant for a glance is as short as its meaning allows; a document people study may run long while every part answers a question. Compress where a glance is enough, and spell out where a misread costs money, trust or safety: prices, terms, instructions.
- **The end is the next step**, never a moral, and never the opening said again.

## The word

- **Plain.** The shortest familiar word, and people with their actions instead of chains of abstract nouns. A needed term stays, explained where it first appears, one at a time, and the reader's own professional terms stay as they are. Plain is not childish: the tone stays adult and the precision whole.
- **People and actions.** The subject acts and the verb is alive; verbal nouns and the passive hide who does what. A sentence you could film is clear, and more than two or three things to hold in one is a cue to split it. An abstract idea gets one comparison from everyday life when it explains more than a paragraph would: one at a time, never for decoration.
- **Exact.** The word that fits exactly, chosen from four or five candidates tried in place, each losing for a named reason. A word brings its associations along, so read what else it says to this reader before choosing it. One concept keeps one agreed name everywhere: a second name makes the reader ask whether there are two things, and a new word for what already has one (newspeak) makes them decode it. A name inside a product describes; the product's own name and line may trade description for memory when they carry its soul.
- **Fresh.** A line any competitor could sign word for word is a cliché, and so is the first answer a model gives about any product ("curated", "seamless", "meaningful connections"). Name what only this thing does, a few steps past the obvious; value words differ at a glance and still sound together.
- **Native.** Words as people of that language say them, in its own formats (the order of a date, the separators of a number). A phrase translated word by word, a calque, a needless borrowing or an abbreviation nobody uses reads as foreign.
- **Stated.** A text that decides says what is so ("we do X", not "I propose X"), with no hedge in every sentence; a real doubt is said once, where it applies. The affirmative ("forgot") beats "not" with the opposite.
- **Specific.** What happens, not the category it belongs to: an amount, a time, a place, a file, a name, the next step; never "later", "data", "operation" or "successfully".
- **Stop words.** Ask of each word what is lost if it goes. An intensifier hides a missing fact, a cliché missing work, an introduction ("it is worth noting") the point, a judgement without a criterion ("useful links") the thing itself.

## The sound

- **Respect.** No "you must", no moralising, no blame in an error, no guilt in something overdue, no pleading ("it's important to us"), no false urgency, no bowing and scraping. The reader keeps control and sees their next step.
- **One voice, the tone by the moment.** A product speaks as one person on every screen and channel, calmer in an error, warmer in a congratulation. One grammatical person and one form of address; the reader is addressed directly where the text makes them a personal promise. The voice comes from the product's soul (kk-look), and humour, where the soul allows it, is with the reader, never at them, and never costs clarity.
- **The product's own words decide.** Its reader, register, agreed names, banned words and house rules live with it (a style guide in the project, or its design system's voice canon): read them first, and record a new ruling there. When a rule blocks the right word, the rule changes.
- **Rhythm.** After cutting, sentences come out the same length and the text ticks like a metronome: vary the length, link the sentences. Beauty comes from an exact thought, a scene and a rhythm, not from decoration.
- **Weight by position.** The strong word ends the sentence and the strongest idea ends the paragraph. Emphasis scattered for effect (bold, underlines, capitals, exclamation marks) reads as shouting, or as machine writing.

## The tells of machine writing

- **Machine text shows in clusters**: contrasts set up to be knocked down ("not X but Y"), everything in threes, a question the text answers itself, a pointer instead of the point ("here's what matters"), a closing moral or metaphor, stock openers and transitions ("moreover"), unasked-for comfort, stacked hedges, a trailing clause that praises the fact before it, "serves as" for "is", inflated words ("pivotal", "robust", "seamless", "landscape"), synonyms swapped to dodge the right word, bold first words in every bullet, emoji rubrics, summary blocks, paragraphs of one length. In English a dash used for punch is one more; in Russian the dash, «ёлочки» and «ё» are typography, not tells. One of these is style; a cluster is a text nobody wrote.
- **The cure is the missing fact, not a synonym**: put back the fact or the plain claim the pattern stood in for, and a detail only this author could know. If there is none, the material is missing, not the style.

## Keeping the meaning

- **The author's text is canon.** An edit changes how it reads, never what it says: every claim, fact, number, name, commitment, condition, quote and distinction survives, and nothing is added. A shorter version that lost a distinction is wrong, and an unclear word is clarified, never just deleted.
- **Change only what was asked**; the approved words around it stay exactly as they were.
- **Meaning is argued in the open.** An input can be wrong or arguable: say so and ask before rewriting, never swap the meaning silently. Quoted words are the speaker's, character for character.
- **Check against the source** after every rewrite: find each fact and commitment of the original in the new text, and recheck the arithmetic and the moment in time on an example.

## Words on the screen

- **Can the system do it instead of the text?** Calculate the amount, show the value, drop the empty section, split the form: text is a crutch where the design could speak. A control that needs a tooltip to be understood is a design to fix.
- **Name by role.** A link names the object it leads to, a button the command or its result as specifically as the moment allows (where a thing goes, not what it leaves; never "OK" where there is an action; an ellipsis when more input follows), a heading what this is, a status what happened to the person's goal, a field what they get by filling it in, and an item in a list the detail that says what it is. Two buttons side by side never say the same.
- **A control reads as a phrase** with its label ("Theme: dark"). Options share one grammar and name only what differs; a checkbox says what turns on, never a negation or a bare "yes"; a disabled control says what makes it available.
- **The reader's world, not the system's.** Their words and their benefit, not our process, our jargon or what is "important to us". Codes and module names stay with support; a term from their own profession stays.
- **Statements and data over labels.** In a sentence that carries data, the facts are bright and the joining words dim.
- **Every state has words.** Success names the object, the result and the next step. An error says what happened, why, what to do and when, and what became of their data. An empty state says what the place is for and how to fill it; a wait, that it is running and, past a few seconds, roughly how long; a dangerous action, its price and whether it can be undone.
- **A message earns its interruption.** It goes out only when the person can act on it or must know now, never for the product's sake or with made-up urgency. Its first words are the news; a toast names the object, and the way back when there is one; a warning worded the same way every time stops being read.
- **Write for the worst case**: the longest real name, the narrowest screen, the longer translation. When it doesn't fit, cut a thought, not letters.
- **Consistent.** One grammatical person, one term for one concept, one punctuation across the product: sentence case, no full stop closing a label, no colon closing a heading. The usual stop words on a screen are the names of screen types ("form"), demands and politeness with no job ("required", "please", "enter", "select"), "successfully", "click here", "learn more", and the builders' jargon ("session", "module").

## Pages, posts and long texts

- **A page sells one thing to one reader.** Its first screen says what it is (the category in plain words), for whom, what it gives them and what to do now, and the offer fits one sentence: whom it helps, with what, why now. The mission and the history come after the reader's questions. The call to action is the action plus what they get; a price stands with what's included and what happens after paying; a value is stated through what the team does because of it.
- **A post carries one thought.** Its first line starts the action (a scene, a claim, a real question), and a long source becomes one thought, one example, one conclusion.
- **An article stands on a brief** (the reader in a situation, what changes, the place, the facts), and its form follows the job: news leads with the event, a story has a hero who wants something and pays for it, an instruction is steps that can be done and checked, a comparison uses one set of criteria. A report leads with its verdict, and its headings, read alone, are the report.

## Numbers

- **A number works only when the reader grasps its size**, reads its notation at once and doesn't stumble on its form. Compare it with something they know (a delta, a trend, a familiar quantity of the same kind), with the exact figure next to the comparison.
- **Exact beats vague**: "seven out of ten", not "most". A range when the precision is lower than a single number suggests, an estimate that would jump between updates rounded in words, and no percentages for what can't be measured: false precision costs trust in the whole text.
- **A number says what it counts**: its unit or label goes with it, in the domain's own unit and the reader's own frame (their time zone, their currency), and a date says what happens on it (due, sent, starts), not only which day it is. Hand over the result, not the inputs: an end date, not the data to calculate one.
- **A number is read as a verdict.** Read what it says on its own before showing it: a small share of a large whole reads as failure, a round large one as an advert. Show the true number with the context that makes it fair, or leave it out; never bend it.
- **The notation follows what the reader will do** with the number: judge it, compare it down a column, dial it, copy it. A phone number on a website is written in full, so the phone offers to call it.
- **Figures** with units, money, months and in a series; words where a sentence starts or two numbers meet.
- **The form follows the locale and stays invisible:** digit groups split the same way throughout (in a table every number from four digits, so the column lines up), a narrow no-break space before a unit, a true minus, dash and ×, ranges from the smaller value to the larger, tabular figures and one precision per column, and units in the column's heading. A sign means one thing, and a norm is a means: depart from it only where that lowers the noise for this reader, as in a dense table.
- **A hero number**, the one large figure a page leads with, is set like an image: large, up front, with its label, its figures fitted by eye.

## Headings and headlines

- **A heading contrasts with its text** in size, weight or both, so it is read first. Without the contrast, nothing else about it works.
- **A heading belongs to its text.** More space above it than below (the Bureau's pages run about two to one), and never less below it than the text's own line gap, or it gets into the text's leading. A subheading that forms one whole with its heading sits at the heading's line gap, and the next tier stays out of the block they make. A second line raises the inner gap, so the space above grows with it.
- **It does one job**: help find, give the gist without reading, or attract. The job decides the form: name the territory where people choose, state the news where they want the conclusion. A signboard ("Our advantages", "Introduction") names a place and says nothing.
- **It is a contract**, with everything under it and nothing else, and it reads clearly out of context: in a list, a search, a notification, a preview. Bait ("you'll be surprised", "5 signs") buys one click and costs trust.
- **Short by information style.** Address pronouns, intensifiers, function words and the rubric's own words go; the subject, the action, the promise and the number that is the news stay, and the fact the reader came for (a price, a count, a date) goes into the heading itself. Wit only where the one job is to attract; where people search or navigate, the plain name wins.
- **Headings of one rank look alike** and share their grammar; read in a row, they tell the story. A row that doesn't add up is a broken structure.
- **The kind apart from the name.** A service word, such as a section or a type, comes off the name as a small contrasting label, and the name stands alone, larger and without quotation marks.

## What the eye assembles

- **Structure is seen, not read.** Before reading a word, people see figure and ground, groups, lines and layers, and a caption cannot undo a grouping the layout makes. The test is effort: the structure the reader needs is visible without any.
- **Every cue votes.** Distance, colour, shape, size, alignment, a shared backing and motion (the strongest of them) each propose a grouping. When they all say the same, the structure looks natural; when they argue, it is chaos. Name the cues behind each connection you need, and find the one behind each connection you didn't mean.
- **Figure and ground.** The main thing is the figure: compact, contrasting, densely filled. Text over a photo or a gradient needs a solid backing, and an important boundary never rests on weak tonal contrast alone.
- **Alike is one.** Similar things group across the whole view, even far apart, so an accent colour on unrelated things links them, and unrelated messages styled alike and set close add up to a false phrase. One entity keeps one style everywhere; what must differ looks clearly different, by position, size, mass or colour.
- **A line leads the eye.** Things on one line read as one whole, and continuity beats nearness. Keep few alignment axes, because an axis shared by unrelated things invents a connection, and put nothing on a line's bends.
- **A backing makes one object.** A closed shape around a group makes it one thing, stronger than its surroundings: one backing, one object. Outlining everything outlines nothing.
- **A pattern holds an object together.** One typeface, one size, one step: an object consistent in itself stays whole even where it crosses other things, and mixing styles inside one object breaks it.
- **Coinciding edges merge.** The edges of unrelated blocks never line up by accident, and an element that must stand out doesn't repeat the rhythm around it.
- **Experience beats all of it.** The familiar (a standard label, a known pattern, a common word) reads at no cost; the unfamiliar is met with suspicion or ignored. Check through the eyes of the people it is for.

## Relations: distance and mass

- **Close means connected.** Each element sits closer to what it belongs to than to anything else; one exactly between two groups belongs to neither, and the reader has to decode it.
- **Mass pulls.** A connection grows with visual mass and falls with distance. A heavy object (large, bold, coloured, on a fill) pulls neighbours from farther away and can take a caption from its lighter, closer owner. Two heavy objects side by side fight, and neither the grouping nor the hierarchy reads. A smaller size and looser leading make a block lighter. Mass and distance trade: a light mark can sit tight to a number, a heavy word needs a space, and both bind to it equally.
- **Inner smaller than outer, at every level:** stroke, letter, word, line, paragraph, block, module, page. An object reads as one thing only when the gaps inside it are smaller than the gaps around it, and the bigger the difference, the more independent and important it looks. "Outer larger than inner" is the minimum; a difference the eye reads at once is roughly one and a half times or more. After every step, go one level up and check again: fixing the lines can break the block.

## Lines of text

- **Reading.** The eye reads in fixations of about a quarter of a second and jumps of seven to nine letters, and scanning takes the first words of lines and items, so the meaning goes first. (Rayner 2009)
- **Size is an angle.** Reading is fastest from an x-height of about 0.2° of visual angle and slows sharply below about 0.15°. A CSS pixel is about 0.0213° on a laptop at arm's length, and a phone held at about 33 cm makes it about 1.35 times larger. Measure the x-height, not the font size. (Legge & Bigelow 2011)
- **Levels.** Sizes and weights form a clear progression, neighbours clearly apart, two or three weights, body text at full contrast. Something secondary is smaller or set aside, never unreadably grey.
- **Line length.** 45–75 characters for reading, a custom rather than a law. Small type needs looser letter spacing, not tighter. (Dyson 2004; Pelli et al. 2007)
- **The stem.** A line's body is its lowercase letters, between the baseline and the x-height line; capitals, ascenders and descenders are leaves. Blocks align by their stems and ignore the leaves; at the scale of a single word the leaves count again.
- **Mixed sizes on one row share one line:** the top of the stems (the x-height), their middle, or the baseline, chosen for the job. A large figure beside its caption hangs from the top: the tops of its digits meet the caption's x-height, so the pair reads as one line; on a shared baseline the figure towers and the caption sinks. A figure inside a sentence keeps the sentence's baseline. `align-items: center` centres boxes, not stems: take the offset from the font's metrics, or trim the line box to the letters (`text-box: trim-both ex alphabetic` where it is supported).
- **Leading is a function of type size, line length and format.** Longer lines need more, and a narrow column of short lines can take a little less; each block gets its own. A baseline grid comes last, if at all: it fixes a rhythm before the proportions exist, and the eye sees gaps, not baselines.
- **Labels touch their values.** Above the field on phones, for long labels and for forms filled in once; in a left column right-aligned to the values for dense panels people come back to; on their own straight edge for forms read to learn what is asked.
- **Text in a control** sits slightly below the mathematical centre of its lowercase letters: exactly centred it looks high, pinned to the bottom it looks low. Set it with asymmetric padding and check it on an enlarged render.
- **A one-line control is part of a line.** A field or a picker fits into a sentence, and the form reads as a phrase.
- **Ragged text** needs spare air at its right edge, and in a narrow column hyphenation (`hyphens: auto` with the page's `lang`), so the edge is a calm wave, not teeth.
- **Lines break by meaning**, with no short word left hanging at a line's end and no lone word on the last line.
- **Paragraphs** are separated by space or by an indent, never both, and there is no indent after a heading, a quote or a break, or at the top of a column.

## Optics over arithmetic

- **Align what is visible.** Visible edges stand on shared vertical lines, optical strings, and a box pressed to the line is not an edge on it.
- **Hang what is lighter or rounder than a letter.** On the left, opening quotation marks, brackets, bullets, checkboxes, ticks and pluses go past the text's edge. On a straight right edge, quotation marks, brackets, full stops and commas hang, and hyphens by a third to a half. The direct-speech dash, and brackets hung fully or by half, are decided once per product.
- **Round and pointed objects hang.** Round icons, avatars and round bullets move past the string by about a tenth of their diameter, and a picture with rounded corners by 1–2 px, up to 5 px when strongly rounded. These are starting values; the eye decides.
- **Large and display type pulls left**, the more so the bigger and fancier the face: a type size measures the body a letter sits on, not the letter, so the gap before a large letter grows with it. Shift it in `em`, so the shift grows with the size.
- **A button aligns by its edge, a field by its text.** A filled button is one shape; an outlined field reads as a line of text, so its box hangs out by its padding and border. A form with labels in a left column stands on two strings: the labels on one, the field texts and the button's edge on the other. A field's width tells how much to type.
- **Centres and edges are optical.** The visual centre is above the geometric one, the bottom margin is larger than the top, and a picture in a ragged column is narrower than the column, because ragged text has no right edge.

## Format and margins

- **Layout starts with two rectangles**, the content and its format: a page, a screen, a card, a panel. Their relation is set first.
- **Margins are unequal, and the ratio means something.** The top differs from the side (equal ones, a square in the corner, read as careless), and the bottom is larger than the top, which compensates for the content's weight. The ratio follows the format's proportions, the typeface's, or a deliberate stretch.
- **Every fill, frame and rule is a new format** with its own margins. So there is more space above a horizontal divider than below it, and removing a frame changes the module: its leading and margins are set again.
- **A format's margins are larger than the line gaps of its text**, which is why a small format cannot take a large type size with large leading.
- **Margins are measured by the apparent lines of the letters**: the stem of the first letter at the side, the x-height at the top. The text box lies about both.

## Modules

- **Modular layout is the division of rectangles.** Blocks are rectangles that do not overlap, share edges in width and height, and add up to the rectangle of the screen or page. A module need not be filled; the rectangles must read.
- **Emptiness is an element** and a simple rectangle. Emptiness of a complex shape is noise, emptiness beside a dense neighbour is dead, and a white field of its own gives an element significance.
- **Flexible, not cellular.** Screens hold elements of every size and texts of unknown length, and a rigid grid of identical cells strains them. Modules follow the content, on shared lines.
- **Know what each module is for** before cutting. A grid of guides is a ruler for building what is already decided, never the source of the decision.
- **Nothing sticks out.** Nothing is wider than the screen. Information that doesn't fit becomes a line, a pull tab, a turned corner, a sticker over a quiet area, or a zoom in place; a block past the edge is a refusal to solve it, and a block that pops up on click is the lazy answer.

## Anchors

- **The anchor is the most noticeable object of a module.** Noticeability runs from pictures to large headings, then logos, signs and numerals, then text; a paragraph surrounded by emptiness becomes one too. The most noticeable must be the most important, and assembly starts from it.
- **An anchor is tied to a power point**: a corner, the visual centre (above the geometric one), or a side, where long objects and groups go. Air on the opposite side, or lighter objects there, makes the tie obvious. An anchor with no point floats, and the layout falls apart.
- **Secondary objects string onto the anchor** like beads and keep the group rectangular, aligned in width and height.
- **An anchor is whole.** Its parts (a big figure and what it counts, a heading with its subheading and the start of its text) stay together, and light text never cuts through them; a caption goes before or after the group.
- **Any object becomes an anchor** when it grows or gains contrast. After a change of colour, theme or state, look again at what now stands out.
- **Colour both highlights and groups.** One accent colour keeps the noise down, and a colour with one meaning everywhere makes the whole page logical.

## Point, line, rectangle

- **Three primitives.** A point has one focus (a circle, a numeral, a sign, a logo, a face in a photo) and makes an accent; it usually anchors a corner or an edge, and its pull is relative to its neighbours: size, density, contrast, air. A line (a line of text, a toolbar, a row of controls) is the calmest and most economical element, and there is always room for one. A rectangle is a module and a container for the other two.
- **A module chooses one geometry**: a line, with everything on one axis, or a rectangle, with its parts aligned at the top and the bottom. A module in between, a strong line with small sub-lines trying to make a rectangle, is weak.
- **Points are few.** The circle is the most egoistic shape, and a crowd of them is noise with no focus.
- **A line as an anchor** presses against the top or the bottom of its module; a mixed line has its anchors at its ends or in its centre; a lone line across the middle of a layout looks absurd.
- **Tiers.** Neighbouring tiers differ in character (picture and text, large and small, colour and white), and there are few of them. Similar neighbours are not rescued with rules and gaps: merge them or change the character of one. Something secondary goes into a side column or a line before it gets a tier of its own.
- **Obey the module.** A logo or a button takes its size from the height of its module; the columns of one tier share a height, or share a width and let the even rhythm carry the rest.
- **Value decides place.** The block most valuable to the reader gets the room, and an important long line is set apart, not cut.

## Large to small

The order is part of the craft: the words before the form, content before geometry, the anchor before the rest, proportions before spacing, construction before nuances. The words themselves go from meaning to words: polishing the sentences of a text that doesn't do its job is tidying a house that has to come down. First write out what is really there and what each piece is worth to the reader, and say each thing once; editing the text is a layout tool, and an explanation belongs next to the button it explains. Then give each block its geometry, place the anchor, and set the line length, leading and margins. The nuances come only after that: the accent, hanging punctuation, hyphenation, optical shifts.

When meaning and geometry disagree, change one of them until both hold. If a clean alignment scatters one meaning to two edges, change the text; if a "logical" order scatters the anchors, change the construction. Keep at least one alternative in view, and know why it lost. At the end, compare with the original content: a rebuild that dropped something is not done.

## How you know

- **Read only the words.** Hide the design and read the text alone: is it clear what is happening, what it gives and what to do? Then read only the headings, then only the first sentences of the paragraphs: each layer should carry the whole.
- **The newcomer's retelling.** After one reading, could someone new to the subject retell the main point in their own words? Is every term explained where it first appears, is it clear in each paragraph who does what and what changes, and does any hard phrase hide a missing answer?
- **Diff against the source.** Every fact, number, name, commitment and distinction of the author's text is still there, and nothing was added that they didn't say.
- **Read it aloud** as the product speaking to the person: it should sound like speech, not like a protocol.
- **Count the tells** in anything a model wrote or rewrote: contrasts set up to be knocked down, threes, hedges, stock openers. More than one or two of a kind is a cluster.
- **The competitor test.** Could a competitor sign the value line word for word? Then it says nothing about this product.
- **Blur it.** In the blurred render the anchors, and only they, stand out, in the order of their importance, and the eye travels from one to the next. If the page turns into one grey texture, the levels are too close; if two things jump out side by side, they compete.
- **Count the repeats**: each fact, label and mark, how many times per screen, and what share of the text is data. Every repeat needs a reason, and what is left after the cleanup can expose a problem in the structure.
- **Take a cue away**: a line, a frame, a colour. If the grouping holds, the cue was noise; if it breaks, it was carrying meaning.
- **Measure the visible gaps**, ink to ink, and compare inner with outer at each level. Margins in the code and boxes in the inspector don't count.
- **Draw the strings**: thin vertical lines on an enlarged render. Visible edges stand on few of them, and every extra left edge on a page needs a reason.
- **Lay rectangles over the modules.** They tile without overlaps, the emptiness is simple, and each one has a job.
- **Try the variants of the same layout**: a heading in one line and in two, a narrow screen, the dark theme, every state. Any of them can make an accidental anchor or break inner and outer.
- **Before and after**, side by side, at the size people will see it.

## What went wrong before

Seen in agents' work on KK's products:
- Rewrites lost the author's meaning: a claim came back as a different claim, a fact went missing when two paragraphs were merged, an unclear word was deleted instead of clarified and the claim changed with it, and a request to fix one phrase changed two.
- New words appeared for things that already had names, and one value went by two names on one page.
- Value lines were the first answer any model gives, in the words every competitor uses, and names meant to be grasped in a second were abstract.
- A document meant to decide said "I propose", an open question went out as a settled rule, and text hedged what its author knew.
- One language's word order leaked into the other, and phrases translated word by word read as foreign.
- Copy described the machinery instead of what the person gets and said nothing about what to do when it fails; leads and missions came out long and heavy, and were sent back as water.
- A copy document and the live page drifted apart after an edit round, while the report said they matched.
- Decorative underlines and bold read as machine writing; a number stood without its unit, and a date without what happens on it.
- The same fact stood in several places on one page, and a label repeated on every row. Nobody counted, so the page passed as fine until the reader called it noise.
- Pages met every number they were given (type sizes, readable x-heights, few left edges) and were still "too noisy". Numbers don't make a construction: anchors, levels and modules come first, and measured targets check them afterwards.
- A big figure and its caption shared a baseline, so the caption floated low and the pair stopped reading as one line.
- Light text was set between the parts of an anchor and cut it in two.
- Five kinds of text had nearly the same weight, and each row held two heavy blocks side by side. Blurred, the page was one grey texture.
- Labels stood far from their fields, headings jumped as pictures of different widths pushed them, and a control was not as wide as the fields it belonged with. Related things share edges and widths; things of varying width get a column of their own.
- A fill behind every value made a dense panel heavy and broke it into boxes: a fill competes with what it holds.
